# Editorial Pillars

## Purpose

This document defines the five questions every Patterns in Place piece must answer. It is the brand promise to readers and the editorial filter for deciding what is worth producing. If a proposed piece doesn't fit one of these pillars, the answer is to either reframe it or not write it.

If you're deciding what to publish next and only have time to read one document, read this one.

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Most independent publications fail in one of two ways:

- They publish whatever the operator finds interesting that week, and readers can't predict what the publication will give them
- They commit to one narrow topic and burn out within a year because the surface area is too small

The five-pillar structure is the middle path. Wide enough to cover years of writing about US cities and place. Narrow enough that readers know exactly what to expect — and exactly what *not* to expect.

The cost of pillar drift is real. A publication that publishes outside its pillars loses the trust of regulars without gaining new ones — the new readers don't know what they're subscribing to either.

## The Five Pillars

### Pillar 1 — The Place Story

**Question:** *How is this place changing and why?*

**The brand promise:** A reader who picks up a Place Story leaves with a deeper understanding of a real city, county, or neighborhood than they had before reading.

**Primary audience:** General curious public, planners, locals.

**What a Place Story does well:**
- Picks one place specifically (a metro, a county, a neighborhood)
- Tells a multi-decade story compressed into 2,500 words
- Anchors every claim in a specific number from the data pipeline
- Surfaces a non-obvious mechanism (industry shift, infrastructure decision, demographic wave) that explains the surface pattern
- Closes with a forward-looking observation — what to watch next

**What a Place Story doesn't do:**
- Generic "the rise of the Sun Belt" framing
- Cherry-picked anecdotes without data backing
- Conflate "interesting" with "meaningful" (a quirky chart isn't a story)
- Pretend a complete story can be told without limitations or caveats

**Examples:**
- "Jacksonville: How a Mid-Size Metro Quietly Became a Logistics Capital"
- "What Happened to Dayton (and What Other Rust Belt Cities Can Learn)"
- "Salt Lake City: The Sun Belt Metro Nobody Calls a Sun Belt Metro"

**Cadence:** ~1 per month once cadence is established.

**Format home:** Metro Deep Dive (`format_standards.md`).

### Pillar 2 — The Comparison

**Question:** *How does this place compare to its peers?*

**The brand promise:** A reader who picks up a Comparison piece leaves understanding how a place ranks or differs against the right peer set — and trusting that the peer set was chosen rigorously, not selectively.

**Primary audience:** Investors, analysts, hiring managers.

**What a Comparison does well:**
- Defines the peer set explicitly and defensibly (CBSA size, regional cluster, economic structure)
- Compares on 3–6 dimensions, not 30
- Shows the comparison visually (bar chart, dot plot, small multiples)
- Surfaces the one or two surprises in the comparison
- Acknowledges where the data forces the peer set to be imperfect

**What a Comparison doesn't do:**
- Compare a metro to its national average (that's a Comparison-shaped Data Take, not a real Comparison)
- Cherry-pick the dimensions that flatter the headline finding
- Conflate "different" with "better" or "worse"
- Use peer sets that were obviously picked to support a predetermined conclusion

**Examples:**
- "Five Sun Belt Metros That Aren't Phoenix"
- "How Indianapolis Compares to Its True Peers"
- "Cleveland vs. Pittsburgh: A Tale of Two Recoveries"

**Cadence:** ~2 per month.

**Format home:** Metro Deep Dive comparison sections, or standalone Opportunity List.

### Pillar 3 — The Opportunity Finder

**Question:** *Where are the underrated opportunities?*

**The brand promise:** A reader who picks up an Opportunity Finder piece leaves with a list of specific places they hadn't considered, filtered through a transparent lens.

**Primary audience:** Investors, movers, developers.

**What an Opportunity Finder does well:**
- States the filter and the methodology in the first paragraph
- Ranks or curates 5–12 specific places (not "the South" — Charlotte, Greenville, Asheville)
- Explains *why* each place fits the filter, with one anchoring number per item
- Acknowledges what would disqualify a candidate
- Avoids "investment advice" framing — these are observations, not recommendations

**What an Opportunity Finder doesn't do:**
- Hide the methodology
- List places without explanation
- Promise outsized returns
- Cover places where the data is too thin to make a defensible claim

**Examples:**
- "10 Metros Where Wages Are Rising Faster Than Rents"
- "Where Builders Are Quietly Adding Supply"
- "The Mid-Size Cities With the Healthiest Young-Professional Pipelines"

**Cadence:** Bi-weekly.

**Format home:** Opportunity List (`format_standards.md`).

### Pillar 4 — The Decision Guide

**Question:** *Where should I live or invest?*

**The brand promise:** A reader at a real decision point leaves with the data they need to make their own informed choice — not the choice itself.

**Primary audience:** Individuals at a crossroads — career move, relocation, first investment.

**What a Decision Guide does well:**
- Names the decision context explicitly (relocation, first investment, retirement, remote work move)
- Walks through 3–5 specific places that fit the decision
- Provides the one-screen summary of trade-offs per place
- Names the deal-breakers — situations where each place would be wrong
- Closes with a framework for the reader to apply themselves

**What a Decision Guide doesn't do:**
- Tell the reader what to do
- Generalize across audiences with very different needs
- Skip cost-of-living, schools, or commute realities just because the data is awkward
- Pretend a 1,200-word piece can replace personal due diligence

**Examples:**
- "Where a $200k Income Goes Furthest in 2026"
- "If You're Considering Florida, These Are the Three Markets to Look At First"
- "The Best Metros for First-Time Investors With $100k"

**Cadence:** ~1 per month.

**Format home:** Opportunity List or hybrid Decision Guide (1,000–1,500 words).

---

### Pillar 5 — The Contrarian Take

**Question:** *What does the data say that conventional wisdom gets wrong?*

**The brand promise:** A reader who picks up a Contrarian Take leaves with one specific belief revised — they thought the world worked one way and now they have a credible reason to update.

**Primary audience:** Everyone — this is the highest-shareability pillar.

**What a Contrarian Take does well:**
- Names the conventional wisdom in the first paragraph
- Shows the data that contradicts it
- Explains the mechanism — *why* the conventional wisdom was wrong (or right under different conditions)
- Acknowledges what the conventional wisdom got partly right
- Closes with what to update going forward

**What a Contrarian Take doesn't do:**
- Manufacture controversy where none exists
- Strawman the conventional wisdom
- Confuse "uncommon take" with "contrarian"
- Sacrifice rigor for the sake of the headline

**Examples:**
- "The Sun Belt Boom Is Already Slowing — Here's Where"
- "Why the Most Underbuilt Cities Aren't the Ones in the Headlines"
- "Population Growth Is a Worse Investment Signal Than You Think"

**Cadence:** Weekly.

**Format home:** Data Take primarily; can scale up to Opportunity List or Metro Deep Dive when the contrarian thesis warrants it.

## Pillar Coverage Targets

A healthy Patterns in Place quarter ships across all five pillars. As a rough mix:

| Pillar | Share of pieces | Cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Place Story | 15% | Monthly |
| Comparison | 25% | 2/month |
| Opportunity Finder | 25% | Bi-weekly |
| Decision Guide | 10% | Monthly |
| Contrarian Take | 25% | Weekly |

If one pillar disappears for two months, that's a signal. Either the data isn't supporting that pillar this season (legitimate) or the publication is drifting (problem). Diagnose honestly.

## How to Decide Which Pillar a Piece Fits

Use this filter when shaping a piece:

1. What is the *single* question this piece answers?
2. Which of the five pillar questions does that match most cleanly?
3. If two pillars feel equally close, the piece probably needs sharpening — pick the dominant one and cut anything that drags it toward the other.
4. If no pillar feels close, do not write the piece. Either reframe it or recognize it belongs to a different publication.

---

## Pillar Anti-Patterns

Things that look like pillar fits but actually aren't:

- **National macro takes dressed as Place Stories.** "How interest rates are reshaping cities" is a national macro piece. A Place Story is about *one place*.
- **Rankings dressed as Comparisons.** A list of "the best cities for X" without a defined peer set is a SEO listicle, not a Comparison.
- **Investment advice dressed as Opportunity Finder.** "Where to invest in 2026" with implied returns is advice; "Markets where wages are outpacing rents" is observation.
- **Personal essays dressed as Decision Guides.** "How I chose my city" is a personal essay; "Where a $200k income goes furthest" is a Decision Guide.
- **Hot takes dressed as Contrarian Takes.** A Contrarian Take has data behind the contrarianism. A hot take doesn't.

---

## How Pillars Compound

A publication that consistently covers the same five questions over years builds something no one-off article can match:

1. **Reader expectation.** Subscribers know what they're going to get.
2. **Search authority.** Search engines reward consistent depth on a defined topic surface.
3. **Citation potential.** Researchers and journalists cite publications that have a clear scope.
4. **Editorial filter.** A clear pillar structure makes it easy to say no to bad ideas.
5. **Brand defensibility.** "Patterns in Place answers these five questions about US cities" is stronger than any tagline.
