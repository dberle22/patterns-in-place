## A Bit of History

Albany's post-colonized history goes back 400 years to the early 1600s as part of the early Dutch manor system. The name of one the key Dutch families, van Rensselaer, still lives on in the name of Rensselaer County. When the Dutch surrendered to the British in 1664, the lands around Albany were renamed in honor of King Charles II's brother James, the Duke of York and Albany, at the same time that New Amsterdam was renamed too (This is clunky but it's trying to get at the original colonial history, how early names stick with us, and that Albany was an original part of New York State at the same time as New York City).
{Review note, 2026-10-27: Structure: the colonial history runs long against the rest. Suggest one paragraph for the 1600s–1700s. Still missing from the 2026-10-25 notes: the fur trade (Fort Orange began in 1624 as a West India Company beaver-trading post, before the wheat economy), and Rensselaerswyck was the Van Rensselaer patroonship (estate), which is the name Rensselaer County keeps. Verify before print.}

Originally, Albany county was the undefined northern reaches of New York Colony. In 1686 the Albany was granted a city charter by the colonial governor. Between 1772 and 1808, Albany county was subdivided into smaller counties until it took the shape that we still see today. Albany then played a key role in the Revolutionary War. Philip Livingston, Albany native and namesake of the road I grew up on (and a fun fact I learned from doing this research), was one of the signers of the Declaration of Independence. And, Saratoga was the site of one of the key turning points in the war, Burgoyne's surrender. 
{Review note, 2026-10-27: Verify "between 1772 and 1808": I believe Schenectady County was split from Albany County in 1809. Livingston and Saratoga look right (Burgoyne surrendered at Saratoga in 1777).}

Albany started out as a rural, agricultural economy, mostly supplying wheat to New York City. With the building of the Erie Canal in the 1820s, and the railroads in the 1830s and 1840s, Western parts of the state could more easily transport their agriculture, through Albany. This enabled Albany to become a center of trading and commerce, then eventually in manufacturing and a regional hub for banking, entertainment, and retail. Albany's importance in transportation did not end in the mid-1800s, it continued its regional importance with the establishment of the Thruway, which connected the major cities of the state following the historic trade routes of the canal and the Hudson. This eventually was folded into the broader Eisenhower interstate highway network. The region was also home to the first long distance flight when Glenn Curtiss flew from Albany to New York City. 
{Review note, 2026-10-27: Fact checks (verify before print): (1) Glenn Curtiss's 1910 Albany-to-New York flight was a record long-distance flight that won the New York World's prize, not the first long-distance flight; suggest "a record-setting flight". (2) The Thruway sequence now reads right. (3) Possible addition: the Mohawk & Hudson Railroad (1831), Albany to Schenectady, one of the first railroads in the country.}

One of the most important examples of manufacturing in the region was the presence of GE in Schenectady, with much of the city's fortunes rising and falling with the company. Along similar lines, Saratoga Springs was an early vacation destination with the race tracks and hotels built on the cold springs. In more recent years, the region has worked to rebound from falling manufacturing fortunes and changing tastes in travel. The city was also made the state capital in 1797, and since then a major source of the region's jobs come from state agencies. This also means that the city has been home to many important national figures via New York State politics, such as Presidents Franklin Roosevelt and Martin Van Buren.

## The Metro on the Map

Albany, the capital city and region of New York State, sits at the confluence of the Hudson River and the Erie Canal, meeting Cohoes and Waterford. It anchors the third largest metropolitan area in Upstate New York, but it isn't one city with suburbs ringing it. Its four old cities (Albany, Troy, Schenectady, Saratoga Springs) joined together into one metropolitan district. Albany proper may be the anchor, but none of the cities are big enough to dominate the region, and they all play an important role in the local culture and economy. The fastest growing part, Saratoga County, isn't even officially a *central* county according to the federal government.

## What Kind of Place Is This?

While not the second largest city in the state, Albany does give Buffalo a run for its money when you look at the economics of the city and how it draws in workers and residents from other parts of the state. To me, one of the most interesting concepts for countries and states is the idea of the Second City. Generally, when we think of a Second City we think about Chicago as the United State's second city, behind New York City. A less commonly agreed Second City is the discussion on which city in the United Kingdom is second behind London. Over time Birmingham, Manchester, and Liverpool have all laid claim to the title, but for much of the 20th and 21st century Birmingham has mostly claimed the title. 
{Review note, 2026-10-27: Good hook. "While not the second largest city": the comparison is metro to metro, so "second largest metro". Residents drawn from the rest of the state: the migration numbers in the next note, as a line of text (no exhibit). Workers: Lens 2.}

New York State has a similar situation where Buffalo, Rochester, and Albany could make that claim. Based on pure population Buffalo has Albany beat, but if we look at economics and state-wide pull than Albany has a very strong case to unseat Buffalo. Albany outpaces Buffalo in per-person economic output and incomes. This is not just an artifact of Albany having a strong baseline, Albany has a stronger annual GDP growth rate than Buffalo going back to at least 2012. Albany also has lower unemployment and higher labor force participation, meaning more residents are invested in the locally economy. And, unlike most Upstate cities, Albany is still a net positive in domestic migration rate, according to the IRS. While Buffalo was the undisputed Second City for much of the 20th century, Albany is quickly catching up in the 21st century.
