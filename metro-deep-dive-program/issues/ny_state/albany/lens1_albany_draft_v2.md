## A Bit of History

Albany's colonial history goes back over 400 years to 1624, as part of the early Dutch manor system when Dutch traders built Fort Orange as a fur trading post and the Van Rensselaer family established Rensselaerwyck. When the Dutch ceded to the British in 1664, the whole colony was renamed New York and the name of the area around the fort was changed to Albany, in honor of King Charles's brother James, the Duke of York and Albany, tying both ends of the Hudson from the start. In 1686 Albany was granted a city charter by the colonial governor, making it one of the oldest chartered cities in the country. The region was central to the American Revolution. Albany native Philip Livingston (namesake of the road I grew up on, a fun fact I found during my research here) was a signatory to the Declaration of Independence, and the region was home to important battles.

The city was made state capital in 1797, which has become a major source for the region's jobs as a hub for state agencies. Being the capital of New York has also made the region home to many important national figures, with four governors going on to be president: Martin Van Buren (who grew up in nearby Kinderhook and whose first language was Dutch, the only president whose first language was not English), Grover Cleveland, Theodore Roosevelt, and Franklin Roosevelt. The Dutch influence has never really left the region. It shows up again in the Empire State Plaza story later in this piece, and every May in Washington Park at the Tulip Festival, one of my favorite yearly events when I lived in Albany and was my unofficial start of summer. The festival is dates back to 1949, after the city made the tulip its official flower and Queen Wilhelmina of the Netherlands sent 2,000 bulbs as a gift. 

The local farms around Albany grew to become a major wheat supplier to New York City. The region diversified into commerce and trade with the building of the Erie Canal in the 1820s, and the railroads in the 1830s and 1840s (the Mohawk and Hudson that connected Albany and Schenectady in 1831 was one of the nation's first railroads), which opened up wheat markets in Western New York and the Great Lakes region, with their crops moving through Albany on the way to New York City. Albany's importance in transportation did not end in the mid-1800s. The region was the site of one of the first long distance flights when Glenn Curtiss flew from Albany to New York City in 1910, a fun fact that's stuck with me years later since I first learned about it in a History of Transportation class in Albany. When the car took over in the mid-20th century, the region stayed a crossroads, as the state's new highways met here.

Along the way manufacturing arrived, and each of the four cities found its own trade. When it faded, each had to find its own way forward. 
## What Kind of Place Is This?

Like much of the rest of Upstate New York, the region's factory economy faded towards the end of the 20th century during the deindustrialization that hit the north and midwest particularly hard. But Albany had a fallback most of those cities didn’t: state government. That steady floor, plus the state’s bet on nanotechnology that brought GlobalFoundries to Malta, is what puts it in this conversation.

To me, one of the most interesting concepts for countries and states is the idea of the Second City. Generally, when we think of a Second City we think about Chicago as the United States' second city, behind New York City. But New York State is more like the United Kingdom where Birmingham, Manchester, and Liverpool all have made the case for the title of Second City behind London. 

In New York, Buffalo, Rochester, and Albany could all make that claim. Rochester's case is the weakest right now: it trails Buffalo in population and Albany in economic strength. On pure population Buffalo has Albany beat. But on the economics of each metro and how they draw people in from the rest of the state, Albany has a very strong case to unseat Buffalo. The Albany region gains from the rest of New York State, while losing population to other states. *From the rest of New York it gains about 3,600 people a year on net, or 4.0 per 1,000 residents (2.8 from Downstate and 1.2 from the rest of Upstate), but it loses even more, about 5,100 people a year, to other states.* {Confirmed 2026-11-01: IRS county totals, 2018–22 yearly averages, per 1,000 of the 906k ACS population, the same basis as the −1.6. ## The Metro on the Map

Much of today's metro goes back to the Dutch. The early village of Beverwijck and Fort Orange were surrounded by Rensselaerwyck, the estate of the van Rensselaer family, which lives on in the name of Rensselaer County. The original Albany County, the colony's open northern frontier, was split up between 1772 and 1809 into new counties, which include all of the modern metro's counties as well as a few other Upstate counties. This ended with the creation of Schenectady County, whose name comes from the Mohawk word for "place beyond the pine" which is also gives its name and location to one of the best modern crime epics, The Place Beyond the Pines. 

The region sits at the southern tip of the Adirondacks, and where the Erie Canal meets the Hudson River, historical waterways mirrored by the modern day *I87* and *I90* highways. The cities were tied together early: the Mohawk and Hudson railroad connected Albany and Schenectady in 1831 as one of the nation's first railroads.  In the 20th century came the Thruway, opening in the 1950s, which connected the state's major cities: Buffalo, Albany, and New York City by following the historic trade routes of the canal and the Hudson.

Albany is not your typical metropolitan area with a central city surrounded by suburbs, it's really four old cities (Albany, Troy, Schenectady, Saratoga Springs) grown together into one metropolitan district over time. Each city grew up around its own trade and industry. Albany had the capital. Troy was an early iron and shirt collar manufacturing center, earning it the nickname the Collar City. Schenectady had GE. And Saratoga Springs had the mineral springs and race track.

According to the federal Office of Management and Budget (OMB) Albany, Schenectady, and Rensselaer are central counties while Saratoga and Schoharie are outlying. Central counties are the ones that hold the metro's urban core, while outlying counties earn their place through commuting (generally a commuting share of at least 25%). When you look at the metro you can see this in the core population and jobs triangle created by Albany, Schenectady, and Troy (the main city in Rensselaer). Schoharie is a more rural county on the outskirts of the metro area, really keeping itself as an outlying county. Only 16% of its working residents work in the three central counties, which is shorter than the 25% target share that OMB uses to tie outlying counties to a metro area.

Saratoga County played an early central role in the region's story. It became a key turning point in the Revolutionary War in 1777 with the Continental Army's victory at Saratoga and Burgoyne's surrender. Albany, Troy, and Schenectady are relatively close and well connected with continuous population. Saratoga Springs is further north with fewer population centers connecting it to the heart of the region. You feel this when you're driving up the Northway. Past Malta it feels empty until you make it to Saratoga Springs and the historical center reveals itself.

But the county is the fastest growing part of the region and there's a growing suburban corridor along the Northway that connects Saratoga Springs to the rest of the metro area. It's close enough to the main job centers with 35% of its resident workers commuting in to the three central counties, with physical room to grow. That's why Saratoga is outlying instead of central: it clears the commuting test easily, but the urban core of the region sits in the other three counties. Saratoga Springs itself is still a small city (with about 29,000 people), but the county is the bridge between the Albany region and the Glens Falls metropolitan area.
## How It's Assembled

### Where people live

Most of the population (72%) lives outside of the four cities, mostly in suburban towns. This was one of the most surprising findings from this exercise as I was expecting more people to live in one of the 4 anchor cities.

![[e5_top_places.png]]

When mapping out the population, I found that the shape forms a rough arrow. Its head is the triangle made by the outline of Albany, Schenectady, and Troy, and filled in by inner suburbs like Colonie. The shaft runs up the Northway through Clifton Park, Malta, and on to Saratoga Springs. Over half the metro's population lives in this core, on roughly 10% of the land. The most densely populated parts of the metro area are the 4 main cities, as well as a few smaller cities like Cohoes, Watervliet, and Mechanicville. To the south and west, just outside of the center of the triangle are a few larger suburbs too, Bethlehem and Guilderland have around 35,000-37,000 each, and the smaller towns of New Scotland and East Greenbush. These are relatively outer suburbs, though they are still close to center of the metro area.

![[e6b_population_arrow.png]]
### Where people work

Since we're talking about jobs, there's no better place to start than with the decision in 1797 to make Albany the state capital. This is most visible today in downtown Albany in the Empire State Plaza. The state took 98 acres of historic downtown Albany by eminent domain, evicted about 7,000 residents and demolished close to 1,200 buildings. The story goes that Governor Nelson Rockefeller was embarrassed by downtown Albany when the then Princess Beatrix of the Netherlands came to the city as a celebration of Dutch heritage (three centuries after Fort Orange, the Dutch continue to shape the city, even if unintentionally). And so the plans for the Empire State Plaza were set in motion. The whole complex took 16 years to complete at a cost of more than $1.7 billion against an initial estimate of $250 million. With 7,000 residents gone, the Plaza completely remade the makeup of downtown Albany from a historic, ethnically diverse neighborhood, to a central business district with a weakened local community.

The busiest single tract in the region is where Empire State Plaza and the State Capitol are, with about 50,000 jobs, 71% of them public. Close by to this downtown tract are smaller, mostly private, job centers: Albany Medical Center (~17,000 jobs) and the Port of Albany. Past Washington Park and further uptown, the largest concentration of jobs in the region is a 13-tract band from uptown Albany out to Colonie's Wolf Road. This band contains 96,000 jobs, including: SUNY Albany, the Harriman State Office Campus, Albany International Airport, and the offices and stores along Wolf Road.

While the population is spread out, where workers go leans heavily toward the city of Albany and the corridor next to it. 

![[e8_job_centers.png]]

The rest of the metro area has some smaller but still significant job clusters. Rensselaer County has a cluster of jobs in downtown Troy as well as a center in East Greenbush around Hampton Manor. Schenectady is in a similar position with a solid job core in the city's downtown, and outside the city limits in Niskayuna, home to GE's research center. Saratoga is a bit more interesting. The jobs follow the population arrow up the Northway, with job centers in Clifton Park and Malta, home to the GlobalFoundries chip plant. Then reaching into Saratoga Springs, where many of the jobs are located, before continuing up the Northway towards Glens Falls.

Public jobs are not only limited to the two main Albany clusters. We actually see some of the largest public-job shares in more rural tracts out towards the edges of the metro in Schoharie, outer Saratoga, and rural Rensselaer, but on small total job counts. Many jobs in these areas are public sector, such as local schools and county seats.

To combine them, I sorted every census tract in the country into one of seven neighborhood types, so a type means the same thing in Albany as it does in Chicago or Denver. Comfortable suburbs are the areas around the major cities and most of that middle core between the three main cities. Put it all together and the picture comes into focus at the neighborhood level. The tracts with more jobs than residents hold 58% of all jobs but only 17% of the residents, spread across the four cities and home to hospitals, campuses, and state offices. Most of the population live in comfortable suburbs, which have the highest incomes in the metro, but few jobs. Beyond them, the suburbs thin out into exurbs and then rural Upstate. Professional mixed-use and students and young professionals are where working and living overlap.

## What Albany Actually Is

So what is Albany today? It's four old cities that grew together into one metro. The cities are the anchors and the suburbs between them and up the Northway are the body. The jobs lean on Albany itself: the capital, the hospitals, and the corridor out to Wolf Road. It has a well earned reputation as a government town with a strong floor, and that floor, plus a decade of faster growth, is why it looks well on its way to being New York's Second City. Whether its hospitals, universities, research labs, and new-ish chip plant make it more than a government town is the question for next time. ## Methods

**Peers.** I use 35 measures to describe all US metro areas, organizing metrics into three groups: Character, Livability, and Opportunity. Each measure is scaled by how far a metro sits above or below the national average, so every metro gets a profile of the 35 scores on the same scale. I then compare whole profiles at once using cosine similarity, which scores how closely two profiles point in the same direction: 1 means identical, and lower scores mean less alike. This method rewards a similar mix of metrics, and most of the measures are rates, so the match isn't just about the size of a market. 

**Neighborhood types.** Every tract in a metropolitan or micropolitan area is described with 22 measures that roughly follow the Character, Livability, and Opportunity framing I used for the metro area similarity framework. Then a k-means clustering method is used to group tracts with similar profiles into seven national types.

**Commuting shares.** OMB sets its 25% test with ACS commuting flows. The shares in this piece come from LODES commuter flows, so they won't match OMB's figures exactly.
