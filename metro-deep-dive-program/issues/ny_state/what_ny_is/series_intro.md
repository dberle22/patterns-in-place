## Center of the Story

New York has been at the center of the national story since before there was a nation. It was one of the original 13 colonies, but while other states have popped up in the national discourse a few times over the centuries, New York has been a major player throughout, from the first presidential inauguration on Wall Street to becoming one of the truly global cities of the 21st century. New York has always been, as we like to say, "the center of the world." But there's so much more to the state than just the city, and a much deeper history runs between them.

For most of US history, from 1820 to 1960, New York had the largest population in the nation. Since then it has been passed by California, then Texas, and most recently Florida. But the statewide numbers hide two very different stories. The New York City metro, or Downstate, has continued to grow, even at a slower rate. While the rest of the state, Upstate, has been flat overall, with 38 of 52 counties shrinking since 2012. This is one of the keys to understanding the state.

## Two New Yorks

{Placeholder} Population Map V2: tract population density as the base, with CBSAs and REDC regions overlaid. Shows the distribution of population (see WHAT_NY_IS_VISUALS_BUILD_PLAN.md)

**Downstate** is where New York became the center of the world. Ellis Island was the gateway for millions of immigrants and turned New York into a melting pot that lasts to this day. Wall Street carried the capitalist highs from the Gilded Age to the Greed is Good era. Madison Avenue and the TV and radio networks of the mid 20th century helped create a distinctly American culture. The city was a center of the social movements of the Progressive era, the civil rights movement, and the LGBTQ movement. And as the headquarters of the United Nations and the site of the 9/11 attacks, it has been at the heart of world defining events.

Today, the city and its suburbs remain the economic engine of the state, and immigration continues to drive much of its growth. For our purposes we define Downstate as New York City, Long Island, Westchester, Putnam, and Rockland. I'll call everything else "Upstate".

**Upstate's** story starts with the Erie Canal. By connecting the interior of the country to New York Harbor, the canal opened up the West, turned New York into a world center of commerce, and created a string of cities and towns across the state. Those communities grew on manufacturing, and when manufacturing declined, they followed the path of the rest of the Rust Belt.

Many smaller communities were then sustained by state investment, especially prisons, so recent prison closures have hit them hard. We'll use the Erie Canal as a lens to view how these communities have changed over time and where they will go next.

Huge public works shaped both halves of the state, from Robert Moses and the New Deal to Rockefeller, changing the landscape and the imaginations of the people who live here.

This dichotomy of a rural, Rust Belt Upstate stagnating and the "center of the world" Downstate will be one of the major themes I explore. As someone from Downstate, I've taken a New York City centric view of the state. Through this exercise I'm hoping to learn a lot more about the rest of this state and to see where it goes from here.
