# Synthesis Prompts

This note is meant to help kick off the `05_synthesis` section.

The goal is not to hand you conclusions too early. The goal is to slow us down
enough to take a real pass across the charts we already built and ask where the
interesting tensions actually are.

## How To Use This

You can use these prompts in a few ways:

- answer them directly while looking at the charts
- turn the strongest ones into article sections
- use them to identify which markets deserve repeat appearances across sections
- use them to notice when two charts are telling different stories

## Start With The Big Frame

Before getting lost in specific metros, ask:

1. What is the strongest story the full package is telling about housing in `2024`?
2. Is the dominant story "housing is tight everywhere," or is it more like "housing stress takes different forms in different markets"?
3. Which section feels most foundational: Vacancy, Costs, Supply Character, or Overheating?
4. What did we assume would be true at the beginning that now looks less true after seeing the charts?
5. Where do the charts seem to agree with each other most strongly?
6. Where do the charts seem to pull in different directions?

## Vacancy Questions

Use:

- `state_vacancy_map.png`
- `cbsa_vacancy_boxplot_region.png`
- `cbsa_vacancy_boxplot_division.png`
- `cbsa_vacancy_tightest.png`
- `cbsa_vacancy_loosest.png`
- `vacancy_trend_regions.png`
- `vacancy_trend_divisions.png`

Questions:

1. Is low vacancy mainly a regional story, or is it really a metro-specific story hiding inside regions?
2. Which divisions look most internally inconsistent, where some metros are tight and others are loose?
3. Are the tightest metros today also in places where vacancy has been falling for a long time, or are some of them newer stories?
4. What does the loose-vacancy list actually mean: slack supply, weak demand, or something more local and idiosyncratic?
5. Does the vacancy story feel like a leading explanation for the cost story, or only a partial one?

Writing ideas:

- "The US does not have one vacancy problem."
- "Regional averages hide how metro-specific tightness really is."
- "Loose vacancy is not always the same thing as healthy supply."

## Costs Questions

Use:

- `state_rent_to_income_map.png`
- `cost_index_trends.png`
- `cbsa_vacancy_vs_cost_changes.png`
- `cbsa_cost_correlation_heatmap.png`

Questions:

1. Did rents and home values really move together, or did one outrun the other in a way that matters editorially?
2. Which states or metros look strained on rent-to-income even if they are not the most extreme on home prices?
3. In the vacancy-vs-cost scatter, how many metros fit the clean "falling vacancy, rising costs" story?
4. Which metros are the most interesting contradictions: rising vacancy but still rising prices or rents?
5. Does the correlation heatmap suggest that cost growth is mostly about tightness, or is affordability strain now semi-independent of current vacancy?

Writing ideas:

- "Costs kept climbing even where the simple supply-relief story broke down."
- "Rent strain and home-value escalation are related, but not identical stories."
- "A market can look costly without fitting the cleanest shortage narrative."

## Supply Character Questions

Use:

- `cbsa_permit_intensity_map.png`
- `cbsa_supply_mix_top_markets.png`
- `cbsa_supply_vs_growth.png`

Questions:

1. Where is new building actually happening, and does it line up with where we expected supply response to be strongest?
2. Are the fastest-building metros mainly in the same broad growth-belt geography?
3. In the mix charts, where does new supply look meaningfully more multifamily than the inherited housing stock?
4. Does that suggest real structural adaptation, or just a marginal shift at the edge?
5. In the supply-vs-growth scatter, which metros look like they are genuinely building into growth?
6. Which metros still look underbuilt relative to their growth story?

Writing ideas:

- "The markets building fastest are not necessarily the markets with the most transformed housing stock."
- "New supply can be responsive without yet being dominant."
- "Growth is not the same thing as supply adaptation."

## Overheating Questions

Use:

- `cbsa_overheating_hottest.png`
- `cbsa_overheating_still_affordable.png`
- `cbsa_overheating_scatter.png`
- `cbsa_overheating_bivariate_map.png`
- `cbsa_overheating_component_heatmap.png`

Questions:

1. Do the hottest metros feel genuinely overheated, or just expensive and fast-growing?
2. Which top-ranked overheating metros get there because of broad-based pressure versus one especially hot component?
3. Does the momentum-vs-strain scatter produce a cleaner narrative than the composite ranking by itself?
4. Which markets on the still-affordable shortlist actually feel compelling after looking back at Vacancy, Costs, and Supply Character?
5. Are there metros that rank as overheated but still look supply-responsive?
6. Are there metros that look calm only because demand pressure is weak, not because housing is especially healthy?

Writing ideas:

- "Overheating is not one thing."
- "Some markets are hot because prices are running; others are hot because households are already stretched."
- "The calmest markets are not automatically the most compelling ones."

## Cross-Section Questions

These are the most important prompts for synthesis.

1. Which metros show up repeatedly across sections, and why?
2. Which metros only show up in one section, suggesting a more specific or local story?
3. Are the tightest vacancy markets also the most overheated?
4. Are the fastest-building metros actually less overheated, or are some of them still running hot?
5. Which still-affordable metros also look credible on supply response?
6. Which high-cost or high-overheating metros are nevertheless adapting faster than the stereotype would suggest?
7. Where do we see a real mismatch between current housing stock and current permit behavior?
8. Which markets are easiest to explain with a single storyline, and which demand a more layered narrative?

## Tension Prompts

These are useful when you want synthesis writing that goes beyond recap.

1. Where does strong building coexist with strong overheating?
2. Where does looser vacancy fail to translate into real affordability relief?
3. Where does multifamily permitting rise without yet reshaping the stock?
4. Where do calmer affordability signals coexist with weak growth or weak demand?
5. Which markets look like transitional cases rather than clear winners or losers?

## Market-Spotlight Prompts

To choose a few markets to revisit across the article, ask:

1. Which metro is the cleanest example of classic overheating?
2. Which metro is the best contradiction to the simple shortage story?
3. Which metro is the best example of supply responsiveness?
4. Which metro is the best candidate for the "still affordable, but not sleepy" story?
5. Which metro seems most difficult to categorize with a single label?

## Writing Angles To Consider

You do not need to use these, but they may help sharpen the synthesis.

### Angle 1: Housing Stress Has Multiple Forms

Possible thesis:

"The housing market is not sorting into healthy and unhealthy places so much as
different combinations of tightness, cost pressure, growth, and supply response."

### Angle 2: Supply Response Is Real But Uneven

Possible thesis:

"Some fast-growing metros are clearly building, but the current stock still
reflects older patterns and the relief is uneven."

### Angle 3: Affordability Relief Is Harder Than Just Building More

Possible thesis:

"Even where vacancy is improving or permitting is strong, affordability does not
automatically reset."

### Angle 4: The Most Interesting Markets Are The Contradictions

Possible thesis:

"The clearest insights are not always in the most extreme rankings, but in the
markets where vacancy, costs, supply, and overheating stop moving together."

## A Short List Of Good Forcing Questions

If you only answer a few, answer these:

1. What story do the charts tell that I did not expect?
2. Which metro would I feature as the clearest case study, and why?
3. Which chart changes my mind the most when placed beside the others?
4. Where does the simple "build more and prices cool" story hold up?
5. Where does it fail?
6. Which markets feel like genuine opportunities, and which only look calm because something else is weak?

## Final Reminder

Try not to treat the synthesis as a recap section.

The best version probably does three things:

- names the strongest patterns
- highlights the contradictions
- chooses a few markets that make the whole package easier to understand
